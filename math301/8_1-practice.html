MATH 301 · Section 8.1

Number Theory — Factors and Multiples

Quick review

Factors and divisibility

If A = B × C for counting numbers B and C, then B and C are factors (divisors) of A. Equivalently, B divides A without a remainder.

Multiples

A is a multiple of B when A = B × C for a counting number C. For example, 12, 24, 36, … are positive multiples of 12.

Factor-pair method

Test possible factors up to √A. Every factor greater than √A is paired with one smaller than √A. This finds all factors efficiently.

Perfect squares

Most divisors pair with distinct partners. For a perfect square, √A pairs with itself, so the number of positive factors is odd.

Rectangles and factor pairs

With 24 square tiles, there are four different rectangle shapes if turning a rectangle does not count as new: 1 × 24, 2 × 12, 3 × 8, and 4 × 6.

The locker connection

Each locker is toggled once for each of its positive divisors. Only perfect-square lockers have an odd number of divisors and finish open.

1. Finding all factors of 72

Exercise: Describe an efficient method for finding all positive factors of 72 and explain why none are missing.

Model solution: Test possible factors only through √72 ≈ 8.49. The successful tests give pairs (1,72), (2,36), (3,24), (4,18), (6,12), and (8,9). Thus the factors are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 12, 18, 24, 36, 72. Every factor greater than √72 must be paired with a factor less than √72, so the list is complete.

2. Multiples of 72

Exercise: Explain what the multiples of 72 are. Give six positive examples and explain whether there is a largest multiple.

Model solution: The positive multiples are 72k for counting numbers k. The first six are 72, 144, 216, 288, 360, 432. There is no largest multiple because if 72k is a multiple, 72(k+1) is a larger multiple.

3. Bottle-cap grouping

Exercise: Josh has 1159 bottle caps. Determine every possible number of equal, nonempty groups using all caps, and explain.

Model solution: Since 1159 = 19 × 61 and 19 and 61 are prime, the positive divisors are 1, 19, 61, 1159. Josh can make 1 group of 1159, 19 groups of 61, 61 groups of 19, or 1159 groups of 1. There are four possible group counts.

4. Rectangles with area 1159

Exercise: Find all distinct rectangles with counting-number side lengths and area 1159 cm². Count rotations as the same.

Model solution: The factor pairs of 1159 are 1 × 1159 and 19 × 61. Thus the two rectangles have dimensions 1 cm by 1159 cm and 19 cm by 61 cm. Rotating either does not create a new rectangle.

5. Sums of multiples

Exercise: Prove: if A and B are multiples of C, then A+B is a multiple of C.

Model solution: Because A and B are multiples of C, write A=Cm and B=Cn for counting numbers m and n. Then A+B=Cm+Cn=C(m+n). Since m+n is a counting number, C divides A+B.

6. Does divisibility of a sum transfer?

Exercise: If A divides B+C, must A divide B? Justify your answer with a counterexample or proof.

Model solution: No. Choose A=5, B=2, C=3. Then B+C=5, so 5 divides B+C, but 5 does not divide B=2. One counterexample disproves the universal claim.

7. Does divisibility of a product transfer?

Exercise: If A divides BC, must A divide B? Justify your answer with a counterexample or proof.

Model solution: No. Choose A=6, B=2, C=3. Then BC=6, so 6 divides BC, but 6 does not divide B=2. Dividing a product does not require dividing either particular factor.

8. Squares and the locker problem

Exercise: Explain why perfect squares have an odd number of positive factors and determine which of 1000 lockers remain open.

Model solution: For each divisor d of n, there is a paired divisor n/d. Usually the two are distinct. For a square n=k², the factor k pairs with itself, giving one unpaired divisor and an odd number of divisors. Locker n is toggled once for each positive divisor; only square-numbered lockers are toggled an odd number of times and remain open. They are 1², 2², …, 31²=961, so 31 lockers remain open.
