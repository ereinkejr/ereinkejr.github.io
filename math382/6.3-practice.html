Section 6.3 – Uniform Convergence and Differentiation

MATH 382 · Real Analysis · Concepts, examples, and exam-style practice

Quick review

Exam 3 review names uniform convergence and Section 6.3 exercises 1, 3, and 5. The questions here are original exercises on the section themes, not reproductions of textbook exercises.

Uniform convergence

A sequence $f_n:E\to\mathbb R$ converges uniformly to $f$ if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there exists $N$ such that $n\ge N$ implies $|f_n(x)-f(x)|<\varepsilon$ for every $x\in E$. The same $N$ works for all $x$.

Uniform convergence and continuity

A uniform limit of continuous functions on an interval is continuous. Pointwise convergence alone does not preserve continuity.

Why differentiation is different

Uniform convergence of differentiable functions does not, by itself, imply differentiability of the limit or convergence of derivatives.

Differentiation theorem

Let $f_n$ be differentiable on $[a,b]$. If $f_n(x_0)$ converges at some $x_0\in[a,b]$ and $f_n\prime$ converges uniformly on $[a,b]$ to $g$, then $f_n$ converges uniformly to a differentiable function $f$ with $f\prime=g$ (with one-sided derivatives at endpoints).

Proof strategy

Apply the mean value theorem to differences $f_n-f_m$ to obtain $|[f_n(x)-f_m(x)]-[f_n(x_0)-f_m(x_0)]|\le |x-x_0|\sup|f_n\prime-f_m\prime|$. This proves the uniform Cauchy property.

Common pitfall

Convergence of $f_n\prime$ pointwise is not enough for the differentiation theorem. Uniform convergence of derivatives is the key additional condition.

Worked examples

Pointwise, not uniform

On $[0,1]$, $f_n(x)=x^n$ converges to $0$ for $x<1$ and $1$ at $x=1$. The limit is discontinuous, so convergence is not uniform.

Uniform limit but derivative mismatch

On $[-1,1]$, $f_n(x)=\sqrt{x^2+1/n}$ converges uniformly to $|x|$ because $0\le\sqrt{x^2+1/n}-|x|\le1/\sqrt n$. Every $f_n$ is differentiable, but $|x|$ is not differentiable at $0$.

A successful differentiation theorem

On $[0,1]$, $f_n(x)=x^2+x/n$. We have $f_n(0)=0$ and $f_n\prime(x)=2x+1/n\to2x$ uniformly. Hence $f_n\to x^2$ and $f\prime(x)=2x$.

Derivative convergence on a compact interval

For $f_n(x)=\sin(x)/n$ on $[-2,2]$, $f_n\prime(x)=\cos(x)/n$ converges uniformly to $0$ because $|\cos(x)/n|\le1/n$. Since $f_n(0)=0$, the theorem gives the constant zero limit with derivative zero.

Exercise 1: Definition and quantifiers

State the definition of uniform convergence, explaining the order of the quantifiers.

Model solution

For every $\varepsilon>0$ there exists $N$ such that for every $n\ge N$ and every $x\in E$, $|f_n(x)-f(x)|<\varepsilon$. Unlike pointwise convergence, $N$ cannot depend on $x$.

Exercise 2: Uniform convergence of $x/n$

Prove $f_n(x)=x/n$ converges uniformly to zero on $[-3,3]$.

Model solution

For all $x\in[-3,3]$, $|f_n(x)|\le3/n$. Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $N>3/\varepsilon$. Then for $n\ge N$, the error is less than $\varepsilon$ uniformly.

Exercise 3: Failure of uniform convergence

Determine the pointwise limit of $f_n(x)=x^n$ on $[0,1]$ and prove the convergence is not uniform.

Model solution

The limit is $0$ for $0\le x<1$ and $1$ at $x=1$. Each $f_n$ is continuous but the limit is discontinuous at $1$. The uniform limit theorem therefore rules out uniform convergence.

Exercise 4: Differentiability can be lost

Show that $f_n(x)=\sqrt{x^2+1/n}$ converges uniformly to $|x|$ on $[-1,1]$, and explain the derivative issue.

Model solution

Since $0\le\sqrt{x^2+1/n}-|x|=\frac{1/n}{\sqrt{x^2+1/n}+|x|}\le1/\sqrt n$, convergence is uniform. The limit $|x|$ is not differentiable at $0$, so uniform convergence of differentiable functions alone does not preserve differentiability.

Exercise 5: Apply the differentiation theorem

For $f_n(x)=x^2+x/n$ on $[0,1]$, verify the hypotheses and identify the derivative of the limit.

Model solution

At $x_0=0$, $f_n(0)=0$. Also $f_n\prime(x)=2x+1/n$ converges uniformly to $g(x)=2x$, because $\sup_{[0,1]}|f_n\prime-g|=1/n$. Thus $f_n$ converges uniformly to $f(x)=x^2$ and $f\prime(x)=2x$.

Exercise 6: Why a base point matters

Construct differentiable functions whose derivatives converge uniformly but whose function values do not converge at any point.

Model solution

$f_n(x)=n$ on $[0,1]$ works. All derivatives equal zero, so they converge uniformly, but $f_n(x)=n$ diverges at every point. The base-point convergence hypothesis is essential.

Exercise 7: Prove the Cauchy estimate

Suppose $f_n$ is differentiable on $[a,b]$. Derive a bound for $f_n(x)-f_m(x)$ in terms of its value at $x_0$ and derivatives.

Model solution

Apply the mean value theorem to $h=f_n-f_m$ between $x_0$ and $x$: $|h(x)-h(x_0)|\le|x-x_0|\sup_{t\in[a,b]}|h\prime(t)|$. Thus $|f_n(x)-f_m(x)|\le|f_n(x_0)-f_m(x_0)|+(b-a)\sup|f_n\prime-f_m\prime|$. Convergence at $x_0$ and uniform Cauchy convergence of derivatives make the right side uniformly small.

Exercise 8: Uniform convergence does not control derivatives

Show $f_n(x)=\sin(nx)/n$ converges uniformly to zero on $[0,1]$, but the derivatives do not converge to the derivative of the limit at $x=0$.

Model solution

The bound $|f_n(x)|\le1/n$ proves uniform convergence to zero. But $f_n\prime(x)=\cos(nx)$ and $f_n\prime(0)=1$ for every $n$, whereas the derivative of the zero limit is $0$. Therefore the derivatives do not converge to $f\prime$ at $0$.
