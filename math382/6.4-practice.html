Series of functions

A series of functions $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}f_n(x)$ converges pointwise on $E$ when its partial sums $S_N(x)=\sum_{n=1}^N f_n(x)$ converge for each fixed $x\in E$. Uniform convergence means the partial sums converge uniformly.

Uniform convergence

$S_N\to S$ uniformly on $E$ if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there exists $N_0$ such that for all $N\ge N_0$ and every $x\in E$, $|S_N(x)-S(x)|<\varepsilon$. The index $N_0$ cannot depend on $x$.

Uniform Cauchy criterion

$\sum f_n$ converges uniformly on $E$ if and only if for each $\varepsilon>0$ there is $N_0$ such that $|\sum_{n=p}^{q}f_n(x)|<\varepsilon$ for all $q\ge p\ge N_0$ and all $x\in E$.

Weierstrass M-test

If $|f_n(x)|\le M_n$ for all $x\in E$ and $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}M_n<\infty$, then $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}f_n$ converges absolutely and uniformly on $E$.

Continuity and uniform limits

If every $f_n$ is continuous on $E$ and $\sum f_n$ converges uniformly on $E$, then its sum is continuous on $E$ (with the usual relative topology). Pointwise convergence alone does not ensure continuity.

Integration and differentiation

Uniform convergence of continuous terms on $[a,b]$ permits term-by-term integration. Term-by-term differentiation requires stronger hypotheses, such as convergence at one point and uniform convergence of the derivative series on $[a,b]$.

Worked examples

Example 1: Uniform convergence by the M-test

On $[-1,1]$, consider $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{x^n}{n^2}$. Since $|x^n/n^2|\le 1/n^2$ and $\sum 1/n^2$ converges, the M-test proves uniform convergence.

Example 2: Pointwise but not uniform

On $[0,1)$, $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(x^n-x^{n-1})$ has partial sums $S_N(x)=x^N-1$. The limit is $-1$ at every $x<1$, but $\sup_{0\le x<1}|S_N(x)+1|=1$, so convergence is not uniform.

Exercise 1: Prove uniform convergence

Prove that $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\cos(nx)}{n^2}$ converges uniformly on $\mathbb R$.

Model solution

For all $x\in\mathbb R$, $|\cos(nx)/n^2|\le1/n^2$. Since $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}1/n^2$ converges, the Weierstrass M-test gives absolute and uniform convergence on $\mathbb R$.

Exercise 2: Pointwise versus uniform

Find the pointwise limit of $S_N(x)=x^N$ on $[0,1]$. Is convergence uniform?

Model solution

For $0\le x<1$, $x^N\to0$; at $x=1$, $x^N=1$. Thus $S(x)=0$ for $x<1$ and $S(1)=1$. Each $S_N$ is continuous, while $S$ is discontinuous at 1. Uniform convergence would preserve continuity, so convergence is not uniform.

Exercise 3: Uniform Cauchy estimate

Use a tail bound to show that $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}x^n/n^2$ converges uniformly on $[-1,1]$.

Model solution

For $q\ge p$ and $x\in[-1,1]$, $|\sum_{n=p}^{q}x^n/n^2|\le\sum_{n=p}^{q}1/n^2\le\sum_{n=p}^{\infty}1/n^2$. The last expression tends to zero independently of $x$, so the uniform Cauchy criterion applies.

Exercise 5: Failure of uniform convergence

Show that $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(x^n-x^{n-1})$ is not uniformly convergent on $[0,1)$.

Model solution

The partial sums telescope: $S_N(x)=x^N-1$, and the pointwise limit is $-1$. Hence $|S_N(x)-(-1)|=x^N$. Its supremum on $[0,1)$ equals 1 for every $N$, so the sup norm does not tend to zero.

Exercise 6: Continuity of the sum

Explain why $F(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sin(nx)/n^2$ is continuous on $\mathbb R$.

Model solution

Each term $\sin(nx)/n^2$ is continuous on $\mathbb R$. The M-test applies because $|\sin(nx)/n^2|\le1/n^2$ and $\sum1/n^2$ converges. Uniform convergence of continuous partial sums implies $F$ is continuous.

Exercise 7: Interchange integral and sum

Evaluate $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(1/2)^{n+1}}{n+1}$ by integrating a uniformly convergent series.

Model solution

On $[0,1/2]$, $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^n$ converges uniformly by the M-test. Therefore $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\int_0^{1/2}x^n\,dx=\int_0^{1/2}\frac{1}{1-x}\,dx=-\log(1-1/2)=\log2$.

Exercise 8: Differentiation theorem

State a sufficient condition for term-by-term differentiation of $\sum f_n$ on $[a,b]$, and explain why it is stronger than uniform convergence of $\sum f_n$.

Model solution

Assume each $f_n$ is continuously differentiable, $\sum f_n(x_0)$ converges for some $x_0\in[a,b]$, and $\sum f_n\prime$ converges uniformly on $[a,b]$. Then $\sum f_n$ converges uniformly to a differentiable function $F$ and $F\prime=\sum f_n\prime$. Uniform convergence of the original series alone controls values, not derivatives.
