MATH 382 · Section 6.6

Taylor Series · Understanding Analysis (2nd ed.)

Exam preparation

Quick review

The degree-\(n\) Taylor polynomial of a function centered at \(a\) is

\[S_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k.\]

The Taylor series is the corresponding infinite series, when defined. At \(a=0\), it is called a Maclaurin series. The remainder is \(E_n(x)=f(x)-S_n(x)\). A Taylor series represents \(f(x)\) precisely when \(E_n(x)\to 0\) at that point.

Under the hypotheses of Taylor’s theorem, the Lagrange remainder is \(E_n(x)=\frac{f^{(n+1)}(c)}{(n+1)!}(x-a)^{n+1}\) for some \(c\) between \(a\) and \(x\).

Important distinction: Infinite differentiability does not, by itself, guarantee equality with the Taylor series.

Examples: \(e^x=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^n/n!\) for all real \(x\); \(\sin x=x-x^3/3!+x^5/5!-\cdots\) for all real \(x\); and \(1/(1-x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^n\) for \(|x|<1\).

Written-response and proof practice

Work each exercise before revealing the detailed model solution.

1. Compute the degree-four Maclaurin polynomial for \(f(x)=\cos x\).

The derivatives cycle through \(\cos x,-\sin x,-\cos x,\sin x,\cos x\). At zero their values are \(1,0,-1,0,1\). Therefore \(S_4(x)=1-\frac{x^2}{2!}+\frac{x^4}{4!}\).

2. Find the degree-three Taylor polynomial of \(f(x)=\log x\) centered at \(a=1\).

We have \(f(1)=0\), \(f'(1)=1\), \(f''(1)=-1\), and \(f^{(3)}(1)=2\). Thus \(T_3(x)=(x-1)-\frac{(x-1)^2}{2}+\frac{(x-1)^3}{3}\).

3. Derive the Maclaurin series for \(e^x\) and explain why it represents \(e^x\) for every real \(x\).

All derivatives of \(e^x\) equal \(e^x\), so \(T_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}x^k/k!\). For fixed \(x\), Taylor’s theorem gives \(|E_n(x)|\le e^{|x|}|x|^{n+1}/(n+1)!\) because the intermediate point lies between 0 and \(x\). This bound tends to zero, so \(e^x=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}x^k/k!\).

4. Find the degree-five Maclaurin polynomial of \(\sin x\) and use it to approximate \(\sin(0.2)\).

The nonzero derivatives at zero alternate \(1,-1,1\) in odd orders. Hence \(S_5(x)=x-x^3/6+x^5/120\). Substituting \(x=0.2\) gives \(0.2-0.008/6+0.00032/120\approx0.1986693333\).

5. Prove that matching the first \(n\) derivatives at \(a\) uniquely determines a polynomial of degree at most \(n\).

Suppose \(P\) and \(Q\) both satisfy \(P^{(k)}(a)=Q^{(k)}(a)=f^{(k)}(a)\) for \(0\le k\le n\). Set \(H=P-Q\). Writing \(H(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_k(x-a)^k\), differentiation yields \(H^{(k)}(a)=k!c_k=0\). Hence every \(c_k=0\), so \(P=Q\).

6. Show that an infinitely differentiable function need not equal its Taylor series at the center. Use \(f(x)=e^{-1/x^2}\) for \(x\ne0\), with \(f(0)=0\).

Repeated differentiation for \(x\ne0\) produces a polynomial in \(1/x\) times \(e^{-1/x^2}\). Exponential decay dominates every power of \(1/|x|\), so the derivatives extend continuously to zero with \(f^{(k)}(0)=0\) for all \(k\) (by induction using difference quotients). Thus every Taylor coefficient at zero is zero, but \(f(x)>0\) whenever \(x\ne0\).

7. Use Taylor’s theorem to bound the error of \(S_3(x)=1+x+x^2/2+x^3/6\) for \(e^x\) on \(|x|\le1\).

For \(|x|\le1\), every point between 0 and \(x\) lies in \([-1,1]\), where \(|f^{(4)}(t)|=e^t\le e\). Thus \(|e^x-T_3(x)|\le e|x|^4/4!\le e/24\).

8. For \(f(x)=1/(1-x)\), derive its Maclaurin polynomial and establish the interval where the Taylor series equals the function.

By differentiation, \(f^{(k)}(0)=k!\), so \(S_n(x)=1+x+\cdots+x^n\). The finite geometric identity gives \(f(x)-S_n(x)=x^{n+1}/(1-x)\) for \(x\ne1\). This tends to zero exactly when \(|x|<1\), giving \(1/(1-x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}x^k\) on \((-1,1)\).
