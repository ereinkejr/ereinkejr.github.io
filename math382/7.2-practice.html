Why this section matters

The Riemann integral is defined using upper and lower sums, not by finding antiderivatives. Mastering partitions, refinements, and the epsilon criterion prepares you to prove integrability and understand the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.

From your Exam 3 review sheet: Know the definitions of partition, \(m_k\), \(M_k\), lower and upper sums, lower and upper integrals, refinement, Riemann integral, and integrability. The proof exercises below develop the central methods; consult the textbook for the exact numbered statements.

1 · Key concepts and definitions

Partitions and subintervals

A partition of $[a,b]$ is $P=\{x_0,x_1,\ldots,x_n\}$ with $a=x_0<x_1<\cdots<x_n=b$. Write $\Delta x_k=x_k-x_{k-1}$.

The mesh is the largest subinterval width.

Infimum and supremum on each piece

For bounded $f$, define $m_k=\inf\{f(x):x\in[x_{k-1},x_k]\}$ and $M_k=\sup\{f(x):x\in[x_{k-1},x_k]\}$.

Extrema need not be attained.

Lower and upper sums

$L(f,P)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}m_k\Delta x_k$ and $U(f,P)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}M_k\Delta x_k$. Always $L(f,P)\le U(f,P)$.

These bound the possible area contributions from each piece.

Refinements

$Q$ refines $P$ when $P\subseteq Q$. Then $L(f,P)\le L(f,Q)\le U(f,Q)\le U(f,P)$.

Adding partition points raises lower sums and lowers upper sums.

Upper and lower integrals

$\underline{\int_a^b}f=\sup_P L(f,P)$ and $\overline{\int_a^b}f=\inf_P U(f,P)$.

Every lower sum is at most every upper sum.

Riemann integrability

A bounded function is Riemann integrable on $[a,b]$ if $\sup_P L(f,P)=\inf_P U(f,P)$. The common value is $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$.

Equivalent criterion: for every $\varepsilon>0$, some partition $P$ satisfies $U(f,P)-L(f,P)<\varepsilon$.

3 · Worked examples

Example 1: An exact upper–lower calculation

Let $f(x)=x$ on $[0,1]$ and $P=\{0,\tfrac12,1\}$. Since $f$ increases, $m_1=0,m_2=\tfrac12$ and $M_1=\tfrac12,M_2=1$. Thus $L(f,P)=\tfrac14$, $U(f,P)=\tfrac34$, and the gap is $\tfrac12$. For $n$ equal pieces, $L=\tfrac{n-1}{2n}$, $U=\tfrac{n+1}{2n}$, and the gap $1/n\to0$.

Example 2: A step function is integrable

Let $f(x)=0$ for $x<\tfrac12$ and $f(x)=1$ for $x\ge\tfrac12$ on $[0,1]$. Choose a partition with a short subinterval of length $\delta$ immediately to the left of $\tfrac12$. All other subintervals have zero oscillation, and $U-L\le\delta$. Hence $f$ is integrable and $\int_0^1 f=\tfrac12$.

Example 3: A bounded function that is not integrable

Let $f(x)=1$ for rational $x$ and $0$ for irrational $x$ on $[0,1]$. Every nondegenerate subinterval contains both types of numbers, so every $m_k=0$ and $M_k=1$. Thus $L(f,P)=0$ and $U(f,P)=1$ for every $P$. The function is not Riemann integrable.

Example 4: Refinement and proof structure

If one interval $[u,v]$ is split at $c$, then $m_{[u,v]}\le m_{[u,c]},m_{[c,v]}$, so $m_{[u,v]}(v-u)\le m_{[u,c]}(c-u)+m_{[c,v]}(v-c)$. Similarly, the upper contribution cannot increase. Repeating proves the refinement inequalities.

5 · Written and proof practice

Try each problem before opening the detailed solution.

Exercise 1: Compute sums for a nonuniform partition

Problem. For $f(x)=x^2$ on $[0,2]$, calculate $L(f,P)$ and $U(f,P)$ for $P=\{0,\tfrac12,2\}$.

Model solution. Because $f$ is increasing, $m_1=0$, $M_1=1/4$, $m_2=1/4$, $M_2=4$. Thus $L=0(1/2)+(1/4)(3/2)=3/8$ and $U=(1/4)(1/2)+4(3/2)=1/8+6=49/8$.
Exercise 2: Prove refinement monotonicity

Problem. Prove that if $Q$ refines $P$, then $L(f,P)\le L(f,Q)$ and $U(f,Q)\le U(f,P)$.

Model solution. It suffices to insert one point $c$ in an interval $[u,v]$. The infimum on either smaller interval is at least the infimum on $[u,v]$, so the two new lower contributions sum to at least $m_{[u,v]}(v-u)$. Each smaller supremum is at most the old supremum, so the new upper contributions sum to at most $M_{[u,v]}(v-u)$. Repeated insertions give the result.
Exercise 3: Prove the cross-partition inequality

Problem. For arbitrary partitions $P,Q$, prove $L(f,P)\le U(f,Q)$.

Model solution. Let $R=P\cup Q$, a common refinement. By refinement monotonicity, $L(f,P)\le L(f,R)\le U(f,R)\le U(f,Q)$.
Exercise 4: Prove the epsilon criterion

Problem. Prove that a bounded function is Riemann integrable if and only if for every $\varepsilon>0$ some partition $P$ satisfies $U(f,P)-L(f,P)<\varepsilon$.

Model solution. Let $I_-=\sup_P L(f,P)$ and $I_+=\inf_P U(f,P)$. If $I_-=I_+=I$, choose partitions $P_1,P_2$ with $L(f,P_1)>I-\varepsilon/2$ and $U(f,P_2)<I+\varepsilon/2$. Their common refinement $P$ has $U(f,P)-L(f,P)<\varepsilon$. Conversely, $0\le I_+-I_-\le U(f,P)-L(f,P)<\varepsilon$ for each $\varepsilon>0$, so $I_+=I_-$.
Exercise 5: Show that an increasing function is integrable

Problem. Let $f$ be increasing on $[a,b]$. Prove it is Riemann integrable.

Model solution. Choose the uniform partition with $n$ subintervals of width $h=(b-a)/n$. Since $f$ increases, $U-L=\sum_{k=1}^n(f(x_k)-f(x_{k-1}))h=h(f(b)-f(a))\to0$. Apply the epsilon criterion.
Exercise 6: Prove a continuous function is integrable

Problem. Use uniform continuity to show that a continuous $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R$ is Riemann integrable.

Model solution. Continuity on the compact interval implies uniform continuity. Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $\delta>0$ such that $|x-y|<\delta$ implies $|f(x)-f(y)|<\varepsilon/(b-a)$ (assuming $a<b$). Choose a partition with mesh less than $\delta$. Then $M_k-m_k\le\varepsilon/(b-a)$ and $U-L\le\sum_k[\varepsilon/(b-a)]\Delta x_k=\varepsilon$. Choosing a smaller tolerance yields a strict inequality.
Exercise 7: A one-point discontinuity

Problem. Let $f(x)=1$ at $x=1/2$ and $0$ elsewhere on $[0,1]$. Prove integrability and find the integral.

Model solution. Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose partition points enclosing $1/2$ in an interval (or two adjacent intervals) of total width less than $\varepsilon$. Every other subinterval has supremum and infimum zero. Thus $L=0$ and $0\le U<\varepsilon$. Therefore the integral is zero.
Exercise 8: A nonintegrable function

Problem. Let $f(x)=1$ on rational $x$ and $0$ on irrational $x$, for $x\in[0,1]$. Show that it is not Riemann integrable.

Model solution. Every interval of positive length contains rational and irrational numbers. Thus each $m_k=0$ and $M_k=1$, giving $L(f,P)=0$ and $U(f,P)=1$ for every partition. The lower integral is zero and upper integral one, so they are unequal.
