MATH 382 · Section 8.4

Exponential Function, Logarithm, and Factorial · Concepts, examples, and proof practice

Exam 3 review emphasizes the natural logarithm and natural exponential. Factorial and exponential-series material here is additional Section 8.4 practice.

Key concepts

Natural logarithm

Define \(L(x)=\int_1^x\frac{1}{t}\,dt\) for \(x>0\). The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus gives \(L'(x)=1/x>0\), so \(L\) is strictly increasing. The integral identity gives \(L(xy)=L(x)+L(y)\).

Exponential as an inverse

The map \(L:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb R\) is onto and strictly increasing. Define \(E=L^{-1}\). Then \(E(0)=1\), \(E(x+y)=E(x)E(y)\), and \(E'(x)=E(x)\). We write \(E(x)=e^x\).

Derivatives and identities

For \(x>0\), \(\frac{d}{dx}\ln x=1/x\). For real \(x\), \(\frac{d}{dx}e^x=e^x\). More generally, \(\frac{d}{dx}\ln|x|=1/x\) when \(x\ne0\).

Factorial and exponential series

Define \(0!=1\) and \(n!=1\cdot2\cdots n\) for \(n\ge1\). The series \(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^n}{n!}\) converges absolutely for every real \(x\) by the ratio test and represents \(e^x\).

Growth and comparison

For every fixed integer \(m\ge0\), \(\lim_{x\to\infty}x^m/e^x=0\). The exponential eventually dominates every polynomial. Conversely, logarithms grow more slowly than positive powers.

Factorial estimates

The ratio \(\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\) often simplifies for terms containing \(n!\). For example, for \(a_n=x^n/n!\), the ratio of absolute values is \(|x|/(n+1)\), which tends to zero.

Visual connection: logarithm and exponential

The exponential and logarithm are inverse functions, so their graphs are reflections across the line \(y=x\). Both are strictly increasing.

Worked examples

1. Logarithm product rule

For \(x,y>0\), let \(H(x)=L(xy)-L(x)\) with \(y\) fixed. Then \(H'(x)=y/(xy)-1/x=0\), so \(H\) is constant. At \(x=1\), \(H(1)=L(y)\). Thus \(L(xy)=L(x)+L(y)\).

2. Derivative of the inverse

If \(E=L^{-1}\), then \(L(E(x))=x\). Differentiate: \(L'(E(x))E'(x)=1\), hence \(E'(x)=E(x)\).

3. Convergence of the exponential series

For \(a_n=x^n/n!\), \(\left|a_{n+1}/a_n\right|=|x|/(n+1)\to0\) for every fixed real \(x\). Therefore the series has infinite radius of convergence.

4. A factorial limit

For \(b_n=n^3/n!\), \(b_{n+1}/b_n=(n+1)^2/n^3\to0\). The positive sequence is eventually decreasing geometrically, and hence \(b_n\to0\).

Written-response and proof exercises

Try each problem before revealing its solution.

Exercise 1: Prove the logarithm product identity

Using the integral definition, prove \(L(xy)=L(x)+L(y)\) for \(x,y>0\).

Show detailed model solution

Fix y>0 and set \(H(x)=L(xy)-L(x)\). By FTC and the chain rule, \(H'(x)=y/(xy)-1/x=0\). Hence H is constant on \((0,\infty)\). At x=1, \(H(1)=L(y)-L(1)=L(y)\), proving the identity.

Exercise 2: Show the logarithm is onto

Show that \(L(x)=\int_1^x dt/t\) takes arbitrarily large positive and negative values.

Show detailed model solution

Since \(L(2)>0\), the product identity implies \(L(2^n)=nL(2)\to\infty\). Also \(L(2^{-n})=-nL(2)\to-\infty\). Continuity and strict monotonicity imply the range is all of \(\mathbb R\).

Exercise 3: Differentiate the exponential

Define E as the inverse of L. Prove \(E'(x)=E(x)\).

Show detailed model solution

Because \(L'(t)=1/t\ne0\), the inverse function theorem applies. Differentiating \(L(E(x))=x\) gives \(E'(x)/E(x)=1\). Thus \(E'(x)=E(x)\).

Exercise 4: Establish the exponential addition identity

Prove \(E(x+y)=E(x)E(y)\) for all real x,y.

Show detailed model solution

Let a=E(x), b=E(y), so a,b>0. Then \(L(ab)=L(a)+L(b)=x+y\). Applying E to both sides yields \(ab=E(x+y)\).

Exercise 5: Prove factorial-series convergence

Prove \(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^n/n!\) converges absolutely for every real x.

Show detailed model solution

For fixed x, the ratio of absolute successive terms is \(|x|/(n+1)\to0\). The ratio test therefore establishes absolute convergence for all x. The radius of convergence is infinite.

Exercise 6: Identify the exponential series

Let \(S(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^n/n!\). Show that \(S'=S\) and \(S(0)=1\), and explain why \(S(x)=e^x\).

Show detailed model solution

The series has infinite radius of convergence, so termwise differentiation is valid: \(S'(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}nx^{n-1}/n!=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}x^k/k!=S(x)\). Also S(0)=1. Since \(E'=E\) and E(0)=1, the derivative of \(S(x)e^{-x}\) is zero and its value at 0 is 1. Hence S=E.

Exercise 7: A limit involving factorials

Prove that \(n^4/n!\to0\) as \(n\to\infty\).

Show detailed model solution

Let \(a_n=n^4/n!\). Then \(a_{n+1}/a_n=(n+1)^3/n^4\to0\). For sufficiently large n this ratio is at most 1/2, so \(a_n\le a_N(1/2)^{n-N}\to0\).

Exercise 8: Compare exponential and polynomial growth

Prove \(x^3/e^x\to0\) as \(x\to\infty\), without relying on repeated L’Hôpital applications.

Show detailed model solution

For x>0, the exponential series has nonnegative terms, so \(e^x\ge x^4/4!\). Consequently \(0\le x^3/e^x\le 24/x\to0\). The squeeze theorem completes the proof.
